[Kunisaki Story 09] When Systems Collide: Power, Destruction, and Memory

By the time the Rokugo Manzan system reached its peak, the broader political landscape of Japan was undergoing a profound transformation.

The balance that had allowed religious, economic, and local systems to coexist
was beginning to shift. The rise of warrior rule introduced a different logic of power. Authority was no longer defined primarily through networks of ritual and patronage, but through direct control of land, resources, and military force.

In this emerging framework, large-scale religious networks were no longer simply partners or intermediaries. They could also be seen as obstacles.

Systems such as Rokugo Manzan — with their economic base, regional influence,
and relative autonomy — did not always align easily with the priorities of territorial rule. This shift was not unique to Kunisaki.
Across Japan, similar tensions became increasingly visible.

One of the most widely cited examples is the destruction of Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei in 1571, where a major religious center was treated as a political and military threat.

Such events were not isolated acts of hostility, but expressions of a broader transition: from distributed authority to centralized, territorially defined control. This transformation eventually reached the Kunisaki Peninsula.
The key figure associated with this shift in the region is Otomo Sorin.

Sorin is often remembered as a Christian daimyo, but his actions are better understood in terms of political and administrative strategy rather than purely religious conviction. In his earlier years, he maintained cooperative relationships with many religious institutions, including those associated with Rokugo Manzan.

However, as the demands of territorial governance intensified, the existence of large, economically independent temple networks came to be seen as a structural complication.

In this context, policies toward religious institutions shifted — at times abruptly.

What followed, according to both records and later tradition, was a series of destructive actions affecting temples across the peninsula. Large temple complexes, particularly those concentrated in single locations, were especially vulnerable. Wooden structures that had taken generations to build could be lost in a matter of hours.

In some cases, there are accounts of resistance — of practitioners retreating to fortified sites or attempting to defend their grounds. In others, communities dispersed, and activities continued in more fragmented forms. The extent and details of these events vary across sources.

But what is clear is that the system as it had existed — integrated, expansive, and relatively stable — was no longer sustainable under the new conditions. Yet even in destruction, not everything disappeared.

Stone steps, carved Buddhas, and other elements embedded in the landscape remained. Unlike wooden structures, they were not easily erased by fire. These material traces became carriers of memory — silent records of what had once existed.

In this sense, the “stone culture” of Kunisaki was not only an artistic or religious expression, but also a form of continuity. It preserved the presence of the system beyond its institutional collapse.

What had been a living network was transformed — not entirely lost, but no longer functioning in the same way. The next phase would not be a restoration of what had been, but a reconfiguration under different conditions.
