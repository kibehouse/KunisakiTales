[Kunisaki Story 03] Adopting a System: Governance, Buddhism, and Indirect Rule

By the time regional cultures had taken shape across the archipelago, a new question began to emerge:
How could these diverse communities be organized into a larger system?

The answer did not originate within the islands alone.

Across the sea, the Korean Peninsula was home to established states such as Baekje and Goguryeo, where systems of governance, religion, and administration
had already developed to a high degree.

Through diplomacy, exchange, and at times conflict, the Yamato polity came into close contact with these systems. This contact intensified in periods of upheaval on the peninsula, when groups of migrants—including monks, scholars, artisans, and officials — relocated to the archipelago. They brought not only technical skills, but also institutional knowledge: methods of record-keeping, calendrical systems, religious doctrine, and models of governance.

For the Yamato leadership, this presented a rare opportunity. Rather than developing such systems gradually from within, they could adopt and adapt an already mature framework. Among the most significant elements introduced were Buddhism and the ritsuryo system — a structured approach to governance based on law, taxation, and administration.

However, the implementation of this system in Japan differed from its continental counterparts. In theory, the ritsuryo system was highly centralized. In practice, it had to operate across a landscape already organized into strong regional units. 

Direct control was neither feasible nor desirable. Instead, Yamato developed a strategy of indirect rule Local elites were incorporated into the system as officials, allowing them to retain a degree of authority while aligning themselves with the central polity.

In modern terms, this arrangement resembled a federated structure — or even a network of semi-autonomous entities operating under a shared framework. This approach reduced resistance while enabling the gradual integration of diverse regions. 

Yet such a system required more than administrative mechanisms alone. It also needed a shared symbolic and cultural language capable of connecting local communities to a broader order. Buddhism played a crucial role in this regard.

As a universal system of thought and ritual, it provided a framework that could coexist with local beliefs while also linking them to a wider cosmology. In regions such as Usa and Kunisaki, where strong local traditions already existed, this did not result in replacement.

Instead, it initiated a process of negotiation and integration — between local practices and imported systems, between autonomy and centralization. The outcome was not a uniform state, but a layered structure in which multiple systems coexisted: local cultural frameworks, regional religious centers, and an emerging central authority.

It is within this context that the encounter between Usa Shrine and mountain-based Buddhism must be understood. What appears, at first glance, as a moment of religious fusion was, in fact, part of a broader process: the alignment of multiple systems into a flexible and adaptive network.
