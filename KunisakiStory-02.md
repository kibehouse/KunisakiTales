[Kunisaki Story 02] Across the Sea: Layering of Cultures

Human presence in Kunisaki dates back to the late Paleolithic period, and by the Jomon era, people had already begun to settle in the region.

With its mild climate, abundant marine and forest resources, and access to obsidian from nearby islands, the peninsula offered a highly favorable environment for early communities.

If anything, Kunisaki was one of many viable places to live. It was not yet exceptional.

A major shift began during the Yayoi period, when new groups arrived from the Korean Peninsula.

They brought with them rice cultivation, metal tools, and systems of knowledge related to time, agriculture, and coordination.

These were not simply new techniques. They represented a different way of organizing society.

Yet what followed was not a process of replacement.

The existing lifeways of the Jomon and early local communities were not erased.
Instead, they interacted with these incoming elements — sometimes integrating them, sometimes adapting them, and sometimes maintaining older practices alongside the new.

Over time, this process gave rise to distinct regional cultures across the Japanese archipelago.

Each region developed its own configuration, shaped by local conditions while sharing certain common elements.

Kunisaki, located close to northern Kyushu, was part of this early wave of transformation.

The local traditions of ritual and belief gradually incorporated new practices and ideas, forming a cultural layer that would later support the emergence of Usa Shrine and the Rokugo Manzan system.

At the same time, another process was beginning to take shape.

Among the many regional powers, the Yamato polity began to develop a different kind of ambition — not merely to coexist, but to organize.

Influenced by interactions with the more centralized states of the Korean Peninsula, Yamato leaders started to explore how a wider territory could be governed as a single system.

However, this did not immediately erase regional diversity.

Powerful cultural centers such as Izumo, Hyuga, and Usa continued to maintain their own traditions and authority.

Kunisaki, as part of the Usa cultural sphere, remained embedded in this layered and plural landscape.

What emerged was not a unified culture, but a dynamic field of overlapping systems — local, regional, and increasingly centralized.

It is within this layered structure that the later development of Rokugo Manzan must be understood.
