[Kunisaki Story 04] Shoen: The Emergence of a Parallel Economic System

As the ritsuryo system took shape, it introduced a formal structure of governance — taxation, land allocation, and population registration. In theory, this system was designed to integrate people and land under centralized control. In practice, however, it produced an unexpected outcome.

For many local communities, participation in the system meant increased obligations — taxes, labor, and administrative oversight — with limited visible benefit in return. In response, some people began to move away from officially registered lands and into less regulated areas — forests, mountains, and peripheral zones where direct state control was weaker.

This movement did not represent a collapse of society, but a shift in how people positioned themselves within it. It was within this context that a new form of landholding began to expand: shoen.

Initially, shoen were developed as cultivated lands outside the strict framework of the ritsuryo system. They provided a degree of stability for those who worked them — access to land, a place to live, and a more predictable structure of obligation. In return, a portion of production was directed to the landholder. 

For local elites and institutions such as Usa Shrine, this created an opportunity. By accepting and organizing populations outside the formal system, they could build their own economic base while maintaining a relationship with the central authority.

Over time, another mechanism accelerated this process: land donation. By nominally donating land to powerful aristocrats or major religious institutions in the capital, shoen could gain protection from provincial officials. These estates, known as “donation-based shoen,” effectively became semi-autonomous economic zones.

In modern terms, they operated as parallel systems — formally connected to the center, but functionally independent in their daily operation. For regional powers such as Usa Shrine, this arrangement was highly advantageous. It allowed them to expand their influence without direct confrontation with the state, while also securing a stable flow of resources.

What began as a response to the limits of central control gradually evolved into a widely accepted structure.Shoen were no longer exceptions. They became a core component of the system itself. In regions like Kunisaki, where geography already supported semi-independent communities, this development had profound consequences.

It created an economic framework capable of supporting not only agricultural production, but also religious institutions, networks of practitioners, and the movement of people and goods. In this sense, shoen were not merely economic assets. They functioned as a foundational layer — a framework upon which social, cultural and religious systems could operate and expand.

It is on top of this evolving economic structure that the Rokugo Manzan system would begin to take shape.
