[Kunisaki Story 05] A Moment of Alignment: The Emergence of Rokugo Manzan

Against the backdrop of these overlapping systems — physical, cultural, political, and economic — a new configuration began to take shape in Kunisaki. In later tradition, the origins of Rokugo Manzan are often attributed to a figure known as Ninmon, remembered as a founding monk who established temples across the peninsula.

However, from a historical perspective, it is unlikely that a single individual was responsible for such a transformation. Rather than a founder in the conventional sense, Ninmon may be better understood
as a symbolic representation — a way of referring to a broader movement involving multiple practitioners, influences, and moments of interaction.

What matters, therefore, is not the figure himself, but the type of encounter he represents. At some point in this process, groups of mountain-based Buddhist practitioners came into contact with the institutional center of the region: Usa Shrine.

This encounter did not produce an immediate or unified system.

Instead, it initiated a gradual alignment between different layers of organization: local ritual practices, mountain-based ascetic traditions, and the emerging frameworks of state and economy. For the practitioners, the mountains and rock formations of Kunisaki were already understood as sites of spiritual presence — places where the boundary between the human and the sacred was thin and permeable.

For Usa Shrine, the question was how to position these practices within a broader and increasingly complex system. Rather than rejecting them or attempting to fully absorb them, a more flexible approach emerged.

Mountain practices were allowed to continue, while new institutional forms—such as temple structures and ritual integration—were gradually introduced. This was not a merger in the modern sense, nor a simple coexistence. It was a negotiated alignment.

Buddhism, in this context, did not function as an exclusive doctrine replacing existing beliefs. Instead, it acted as a shared framework — one that could connect different practices without eliminating their distinctions.

At the same time, the expanding shoen system provided an economic base that made sustained activity possible. Practitioners were no longer isolated. They were now connected — to communities, to land, and to flows of resources. What began as dispersed practices gradually developed into a network. This network was not yet fully articulated. The term “Rokugo Manzan” itself would only come into use much later. But the underlying structure — a distributed system of temples, practitioners, and communities spanning the peninsula — was already emerging.

From this point forward, the development of Rokugo Manzan can be understood not as the execution of a fixed plan, but as the ongoing process of maintaining and expanding this alignment.
