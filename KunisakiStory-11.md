[Kunisaki Story 11] Mountains of the Buddha, Paths of the Journey

Even after the cycles of destruction and renewal, the Rokugo Manzan system did not disappear. It changed form — repeatedly, and sometimes dramatically — but its underlying patterns continued. In the early modern period, the system had already shifted from one sustained by estates and hierarchy to one supported by communities and movement.

Yet another transformation would follow. In the late nineteenth century, the Meiji Restoration brought a new wave of reforms that redefined the relationship between religion and the state. Policies separating Shinto and Buddhism
disrupted long-standing practices of integration. Temples associated with shrines were dismantled, rituals were reclassified or suppressed, and established connections were severed.

In Kunisaki, this meant the loss of institutional continuity — including the dissolution of Miroku-ji, which had long functioned as a central node in the network.

Yet, once again, the system did not vanish entirely.

As in earlier periods, continuity was maintained not only through institutions,
but through people. Objects were preserved in private spaces. Practices were adapted rather than abandoned. Rituals continued in altered forms. 

Over time, as the intensity of these reforms diminished, activity began to return to the mountains. But the context had changed.

What reemerged was not a closed system of ascetic practice, nor a network sustained by estates, but something more open. Pilgrimage, travel, and learning became central modes of engagement. Visiting temples was no longer limited
to those within the system. It became accessible to a wider range of people — those seeking meaning, those interested in history, and those simply drawn to the landscape. In this process, the boundary between religious practice and travel became increasingly fluid. The mountains of Kunisaki were no longer only sites of training. They became destinations — places to visit, to experience, and to reflect.

This shift did not diminish the significance of the system. Rather, it allowed it to persist under new conditions. What had once been transmitted through institutional authority or economic structure was now carried through movement, memory, and shared experience. Even today, traces of these layers remain. Stone Buddhas stand quietly along mountain paths. Festivals continue in local communities. Pilgrims and visitors walk routes that connect sites across the peninsula.

These are not fragments of a lost system. They are expressions of an ongoing process. Rokugo Manzan, when viewed in this way, is not a fixed tradition or a historical artifact. It is a case of how a cultural system can emerge, adapt, and continue across changing conditions.

A system shaped by land, by people, and by the relationships between them. The mountains remain. The paths remain. And along them, the process continues.
