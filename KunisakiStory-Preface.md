[Kunisaki Story Preface] Beyond the Landmarks

It would be a shame to experience Kunisaki only by visiting its historic sites.

A simple travel guide is not enough.

Beyond the stone Buddhas and ancient temples,
there is another Kunisaki — a landscape where belief, nature,
and everyday life have long been intertwined.

This is a story about how those elements came together.


The story begins in the early eighth century,
when the idea of a “state” was only just beginning to take shape across the Japanese archipelago.

Around that time,
Buddhist teachings—introduced from the continent — began to encounter the practices and ways of life already rooted in this peninsula.

This was not a sudden change, but a gradual interaction.

Practitioners lived among rock ledges and cliffs, traveled between villages,
shared religious teachings, and passed on practical knowledge — ways of cultivating land and living with the mountains and the sea.

Over time,
these influences became embedded in both thought and daily life, giving rise to a distinctive culture in which belief and everyday practice were closely intertwined.

Eventually,
people came to describe these mountains as places where “Buddha-filled peaks
extend across six regions,”giving rise to the name Rokugo Manzan.

What emerged here was not a system imposed from the center, but a loosely connected network of sites across the peninsula — one that continued to change in response to shifts in politics, economy, and everyday life.

A system emerged, expanded, and kept evolving.

This story follows that process.
