[Kunisaki Story 01] A Peninsula Shaped by Volcanoes

If we are to understand the history of the Kunisaki Peninsula, we have to begin not with people, but with the land itself.

The countless stone Buddhas carved into cliffs, the steep ridges once walked by ascetic monks, and the rock ledges believed to be inhabited by deities — all of these exist on a stage formed by volcanic activity millions of years ago.

The mountains of Kunisaki are not particularly high.
Yet their structure is distinctive:
radial ridges spreading outward, separated by valleys and fan-shaped plains, all surrounded by an indented coastline.

This geography did more than create a scenic landscape. It created a pattern of human settlement.

Each valley formed a unit of life — large enough to sustain a small community, yet separated enough to maintain a degree of independence.

At the same time, these units were not isolated. The peninsula as a whole remained connected through the sea and through the ridgelines that linked one valley to another. The mountains provided timber and fuel. The sea offered abundant food. The plains supported agriculture.Rather than relying on a single resource, life here was supported by a layered system of land, sea, and forest. In a time before efficient communication, this balance between separation and connection created an ideal condition for a distributed network of communities.

It is tempting to see this as a coincidence.

But in hindsight, the fundamental conditions for what would later become
the Rokugo Manzan system were already present in this landscape.
