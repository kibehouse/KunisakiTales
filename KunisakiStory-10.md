[Kunisaki Story 10] After the Fires: Reconfiguration and Community-Based Renewal

Following the destruction of the late medieval period, the Rokugo Manzan system did not simply return to its former state.

What emerged instead was a different configuration — shaped by new political conditions and by the agency of local communities.

With the onset of the Edo period, Japan entered a long phase of relative stability. This stability extended beyond urban centers into rural regions such as Kunisaki. Agricultural production became more predictable, coastal routes facilitated the movement of goods, and local economies gradually accumulated surplus.

These changes created the conditions for reconstruction.

In the early stages, restoration efforts were often initiated by regional authorities. Under the rule of the Kitsuki domain, certain temples — such as Futago-ji — were supported as important ritual and symbolic sites. For the domain, these institutions served not only religious functions, but also contributed to social cohesion and the stabilization of local society.

However, this top-down support alone does not fully explain the recovery.

A second layer of activity emerged from within the villages themselves. Local groups—often organized as ko (associational networks) — began to take part in rebuilding efforts. These groups pooled resources, coordinated labor, and supported the repair of temples, paths, and related infrastructure.

In modern terms, this might be understood as a form of collective, community-based investment.

Through such efforts, reconstruction became not merely a restoration of structures, but a reestablishment of relationships — between people, places, and practices.

The role of temples also evolved.

While earlier forms of Rokugo Manzan had been closely tied to shoen-based economic systems and aristocratic networks, the renewed system relied more directly on local participation. Temples became focal points for community activity — places where religious practice, social interaction, and local governance intersected.

At the same time, patterns of movement changed. Pilgrimage began to take on a new significance. Visiting temples was no longer limited to practitioners engaged in ascetic training. It also became a form of travel — combining devotion, social exchange, and the experience of movement through the landscape. Groups of participants would travel together, visiting multiple sites,
sharing meals, and carrying back objects imbued with ritual meaning.

Through these practices, the network was reactivated — not through centralized coordination, but through repeated, distributed participation. Ritual itself adapted as well. Events that had once been primarily internal to specific communities or practitioners became more accessible and performative.

In this context, rituals such as the Shujo Onie can be understood not only as religious observances, but also as moments of collective experience — drawing together participants from different places.

In parallel, the physical structure of temples evolved. Some sites were relocated or rebuilt in more accessible locations, reflecting a shift from secluded ascetic practice to broader public engagement.

Over time, these developments produced a more open and participatory system. The Rokugo Manzan network was no longer sustained primarily by elite patronage or estate-based economies. Instead, it was maintained through the ongoing involvement of communities, travelers, and practitioners.

In this sense, what emerged in the early modern period was not a restoration
of the previous system, but a transformation. A network once tied to land and hierarchy had become one sustained by movement, participation, and shared practice.

This reconfigured form would carry the system forward into the modern era.
