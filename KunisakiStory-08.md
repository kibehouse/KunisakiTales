[Kunisaki Story 08] When the System Became a Landscape: Maturity and Circulation

By the late Heian and into the Kamakura period, the Rokugo Manzan system had reached a stage of maturity. What had once been an emerging network was now fully embedded in the landscape and in everyday life. Temples were distributed across the peninsula, shoen estates supported agricultural production, and communities were connected through overlapping layers of religious and economic activity.

At this stage, the system was no longer something external that people participated in. It had become the environment itself — a lived structure that shaped how people moved, worked, and believed. The relationship between mountains and villages also evolved. Religious activity was no longer confined to remote ascetic sites.

Temples closer to settlements functioned as points of interaction, where practitioners and local communities met through ritual, exchange, and seasonal gatherings. This created a continuous flow between mountain and village — not a division,
but a circulation. Practitioners traveled along established routes, moving from one site to another, performing rituals, receiving offerings, and redistributing both material and symbolic resources.

Each movement reinforced the network. Villagers contributed rice, textiles, and labor. Temples, in turn, provided ritual services, maintenance of infrastructure, and a shared framework of meaning. 

In this way, the system operated through circulation — of goods, people, and information. It was neither centrally controlled nor entirely decentralized, but sustained through continuous interaction. At the same time, the network served a stabilizing function. Through its connections to Usa Shrine and to broader aristocratic structures, Rokugo Manzan formed part of a wider web that mediated relationships across regions.

In a period when large-scale conflict was relatively limited in this area, such networks may have contributed to a degree of regional stability. The economic dimension also expanded. Shoen estates in areas such as Tashibu, Aki, and Musashi supported not only local consumption but also regional exchange. Coastal routes linked the peninsula to the Seto Inland Sea network, allowing rice, timber, and other resources to circulate beyond the region.

What had developed in Kunisaki was not an isolated system, but a node within a larger economic and cultural sphere. At its peak, the number of temples associated with Rokugo Manzan reached several dozen, with practitioners numbering in the hundreds or more. Functional distinctions also became clearer: central temples serving administrative roles, training sites for ascetic practice, and temples closely connected to local communities.

Together, they formed a distributed yet coordinated structure spanning the peninsula. At this stage, the system appeared stable — even self-sustaining. Its economic base was secure, its social role well integrated, and its symbolic framework widely shared. Yet stability at this scale often carries its own risks. As political authority continued to shift — particularly with the rise of warrior rule — the relationship between religious networks and territorial control would begin to change.

What had once been a flexible and adaptive system would soon be tested by a different logic of power.
