[Kunisaki Story 06] From Rock Ledges: How the System Began to Operate

If Rokugo Manzan was not formed at a single moment, the next question becomes: how did it actually begin to function?

In its early stages, what would later be recognized as Rokugo Manzan did not yet exist as a clearly defined system. Instead, it consisted of small, localized practices — groups of practitioners living and training in the mountains of Kunisaki. These practitioners did not rely on large temple complexes. Their primary sites were natural formations: rock ledges, caves, and cliff faces that were understood as places of spiritual presence.

In this sense, the landscape itself functioned as a form of temple. This approach differed significantly from the institutional Buddhism promoted by the state, where temples were formal centers of learning and ritual, and religious activity was tightly regulated.

In contrast, mountain-based practitioners operated in a more flexible space — geographically, socially, and institutionally. Their practices were embedded in the environment and not confined to constructed structures. The turning point came when these dispersed practices became connected to the regional network centered on Usa Shrine. Through this connection, mountain practitioners gained access to communities, resources, and a broader social role.

What had been isolated sites of ascetic practice began to form a network extending across the peninsula. This transition did not eliminate the original form of practice. The rock ledges and mountain paths remained central. What changed was their relationship to the surrounding society. Practitioners were no longer only engaged in personal training. They also began to interact more directly with local communities — performing rituals, offering prayers, and participating in seasonal activities.

For the villagers, this presence was not necessarily unfamiliar. The idea that spiritual forces resided in mountains, rocks, and natural features was already deeply rooted in local traditions. From this perspective, the arrival of practitioners did not introduce an entirely new system, but rather reinterpreted existing beliefs within a different framework.

This reinterpretation was not primarily textual. In a society where literacy was limited, communication relied on experience — on what people could see, hear, and feel. Ritual performances played a crucial role in this process. Events involving masked figures, fire, movement, and direct interaction with participants created a form of embodied understanding.

In later periods, these practices would be preserved in rituals such as the Shujo Onie, where figures identified as “demons” appear not as threats, but as agents of protection. The inversion of fear into protection — transforming a source of danger into a source of safety — was a powerful conceptual shift. It provided a framework through which people could reinterpret uncertainty and risk within their daily lives.

In this way, Rokugo Manzan functioned not only as a religious network, but also as a system of communication. It translated abstract ideas into shared experiences. By the time this process matured, the network had already begun to extend across multiple regions of the peninsula.

What had started as isolated practices on rock ledges had become a connected system — linking mountains, villages, and people through movement, ritual, and shared meaning.
